Does gamification actually change how users think?

Two experiments on points, badges, and choice — and what they really buy you. M.S. thesis, Lehigh University.

Products gamify constantly on the assumption that game elements make users more engaged and even more creative — but most evidence is correlational. I built a gamified task environment in Python (points, badges, unlockable stages, performance feedback) and ran two randomized experiments measuring what those mechanics do to cognitive flexibility, creative problem-solving, and motivation.

  • Placement beats presence. Rewards placed between task blocks did nothing — and slightly hurt performance. The same rewards embedded trial-by-trial inside the interaction loop made users respond ~270 ms faster and cut switch costs by 60%.
  • Users optimize for exactly what you reward. Paying for speed bought speed — and cost 5 points of accuracy. A classic speed–accuracy trade-off, created by the incentive design itself.
  • Perceived competence was the most movable lever (d = 0.69), responding strongly to immediate performance feedback while superficial choice barely moved autonomy.
  • Creativity didn't budge on average — but users who actively engaged with the game systems solved 67% of insight problems vs. 47% for unengaged users. Engagement, not gamification per se, may be the active ingredient.

What this means for product design

  1. If a reward system lives outside the core interaction loop, expect it to distract, not motivate.
  2. Audit what a point system literally pays for — that is the behavior you will ship.
  3. Trial-level feedback is a proven mechanism for making users feel capable.
  4. Choice must be consequential to register as autonomy.
  5. Averages hide the story: design and measure for engagement heterogeneity.

About

I came to UX research through experimental psychology: four years across three research programs at Lehigh University, ending with an M.S. in Psychology (GPA 3.87). Along the way I served as lab manager for a cross-disciplinary program studying human-centered cybersecurity, supported usability research on HoloLens AR interactions, trained and supervised student researchers, and ran the operational layers of research — IRB, recruitment, moderation, analysis, reporting.

What that background gives me is a habit of intellectual honesty: I report what the data shows, including null results, and I treat "why did users do that?" as a question you answer with evidence rather than intuition.

Outside research hours I design and build a video game solo — code, art, music, and iterative playtesting — which keeps me close to the craft side of the interfaces I study.
